The flotilla casts off on the flood tide, four heavy grain barges riding low under the weight of pikes, baggage carts, and four hundred sellswords. The crews push away from Alderford's muddy wharves while the last ropes are still being coiled, and for a few breaths the whole column seems to hang between land and water: too heavy to turn back, too slow to outrun the current.

Then the river takes you.

Alderford's timber wharves fall away behind the stern wake, the weir's roar fading into the general hiss of current and creaking hull-timber. The town shrinks by degrees into smoke, slate roofs, and the orange pinpricks of watch fires. From the deck, the great imperial weir looks less like a monument than a scar someone has taught to produce revenue. Its old stonework still holds, but the bronze gates are worked by guild hands now, and the tolls belong to merchants who wear no crown's badge.

Ahead, the Grey River narrows into limestone gorge walls streaked green with weed and old flood-lines, the water running fast and black under a low grey sky. Barges have been carrying grain, iron, soldiers, and prisoners through this gorge for longer than anyone in the company can remember. The river does not care which of those things you are. It takes them all downstream.

Word passes hull to hull in flat, clipped tones: nobody ever cleared the old imperial boom. It was built to close the river against raiders, back when an imperial officer could send a request upriver and expect an answer before the season changed. Now the chain lies drowned beneath the trade route it was meant to protect, a monument to a government that left its hardware behind when it stopped sending people.

Somewhere under this stretch of black water, two centuries of rotted iron still wait on the riverbed. The current combs the surface smooth over the hidden teeth, and every barge in the column carries enough weight to break itself open if it catches wrong. The lead barge pilot has nothing but her own eyes and a boat-pole to find a way around it.

"Eyes on the water!" the pilot shouts down the line, fighting the current with both hands on the tiller. "Anyone who's ever read river or reef, I want you at the rail *now*!"

The gorge walls close in tighter through the afternoon, funneling a raw wind straight off the water and down the back of every collar on deck. The four barges have lost their clean formation by then. One rides ahead around a bend, one falls behind, and the others drift close enough for shouted orders to cross the water but too far apart for anyone to mistake the distance for safety.

Spray comes over the bow in sheets now, and the grey light gives no real warmth to dry by—just hours more of it, all the way to the gorge mouth. Men and women who spent the morning boasting over dice huddle under baggage tarps with their knees drawn up. Bowstrings are tucked beneath cloaks. Firewood is counted and recounted. A cook tries to coax a smokeless flame from damp chips while the river rolls past with the patience of something that has already outlasted every army sent along its banks.

You begin to understand the particular cruelty of river travel. On a road, every mile is a decision made by feet and wheels. Here, the current makes the decision for you, and all your skill can do is keep the surrender from becoming a wreck.

The gorge finally opens onto a wide, brown-water estuary. The change is so sudden that the company falls quiet: after hours of black water and close stone, the horizon has room in it again. Wind comes off the distant coast carrying salt, tar, rotting weed, and the heavy sweet stink of fish rendered down in market vats.

Port Valen rises out of the evening haze ahead—a sprawl of grey slate roofs, stone quaysides, and forested masts crowding a harbor bigger than Alderford and the Black Sinks causeway put together. Warehouse cranes turn above the waterfront like skeletal gallows. Guild flags snap from the customs towers. Barges unload beneath rows of lanterns while clerks in dry coats stand above the mud and decide what every sack, blade, and body is worth.

The city was built to be an imperial port. Now the river still brings the same taxes, soldiers, and grain, but the people collecting them answer to ledgers, contracts, and hired steel. The old authority has not vanished so much as been divided among whoever can keep a gate shut, a scale honest, or a sword pointed in the right direction.

Gulls wheel and scream over the fish markets. Somewhere beyond the seawall, a harbor bell tolls the evening watch. Its sound reaches you across the brown water, deep and measured, marking not safety but arrival: the end of one current, and the beginning of whatever Port Valen has paid the Carrion Company to do next.
