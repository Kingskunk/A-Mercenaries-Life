The road rises through several days of old forest and breaks over a low hill. Beyond it, Langston sits inside a waist-high stone wall. Fresh whitewash catches the last red light on the timber-framed houses, and lamps already burn where the dirt road becomes cobble. The fields beyond the wall are dark furrows and stubble, signs of a town with full storehouses and coin moving through it.

After a long day of roots, ruts, and roadside meals, the promise of a hot supper and paid work is enough to quicken your pace. The first stars have appeared when a wolf calls somewhere behind you. Two more answer from opposite sides of the road. Another howl rises closer than the rest.

Brush moves beneath the trees, keeping pace with you. Whatever is following has numbers enough to be patient.

Maybelline's parents rise together. Her mother thanks the mourners for filling the room for a woman who had filled their lives. She remembers a daughter who was relentlessly kind, generous, and patient—a source of warmth to her family from the beginning.

Her father manages only a few sentences before his voice fails. He says that every lane and doorstep in Langston carries some memory of Maybelline. The couple will close their home for a time and ask their neighbors to let them grieve in private.

Concern murmurs through the room. Anathram answers it herself. "They will have their privacy. My people will find whoever killed their daughter."

Maybelline's father sits with wet cheeks and his jaw clenched hard enough to tremble. Her mother keeps one hand on his arm and invites a young man forward. He holds a crushed bunch of daisies against his chest as though the stems are the only firm thing left to him.

"I'm Tomlin," he says. "Maybelline was meant to be my wife. Before I loved her, I never thought I was the marrying kind. With her, marriage felt more like fate than a choice."

Two men near the wall exchange a whisper. One wonders whether "the Wolf" will begin prowling again. The other advises Langston to guard its sisters, daughters, and wives. Their own wives silence them with matching looks of fury and embarrassment. Several other women glance toward one another before facing the coffin again.

Tomlin closes his eyes and gathers himself. "She loved daisies, the color lavender, and anything made of silk. Work will have to carry me for a while. I can only try to repay the kindness Langston has shown me. My door remains open to all of you."

The room answers with restrained applause and murmured condolences. Tomlin's grief is plain, but the people around him are carrying something more complicated than sorrow.

Roi takes Tomlin's place. He has washed the road dust away, tied his beard into leather cords, and failed to make either improvement look formal. The sight of him draws raised cups and a warm murmur from the room.

"Langston welcomed me when my circle assigned these woods to my keeping," he says. "You have respected the forest and its creatures. In return, I have been glad to offer counsel and keep the wilder forces around us from troubling your homes."

The mourners drink with him. Roi studies his flowers with private satisfaction before yielding the front of the room.

Her rumbling accent and deadpan delivery reveal nothing about what she thinks of you. She gestures for you to follow, then stops to speak with the balding innkeeper, who listens with a cloth folded over one arm.

"The town will pay the traveler's room and board while the work remains ours," she tells him. "Is that acceptable?"

The innkeeper gives you a long look before taking a key from beneath the counter. "For Maybelline, it is."

Anathram gives you the key. "Adventurer. Mercenary. Hero for hire. The name does not matter. My people are frightened and need someone, and you are here. Since you are an outsider, your failure would not become Langston's failure. Hear my terms at the town hall in the morning. Your room and supper are paid tonight whether you accept or not."

"I'll hear them," you say. "Nothing more until I know what happened."

"Sensible." When you start to thank her, she raises one hand. "I do not know you yet, and the killer may find you before I do. While you investigate for Langston, the town will pay for your stay. Tonight, eat and sleep. Tomorrow, decide."

She turns back to Maybelline's parents, stopping twice to answer mourners who catch her sleeve.

Tomlin approaches after her. Up close, sleep and crying have reddened his striking eyes.

"Hello. We haven't met," he says. "How did you know Maybelline?"

"I didn't. I reached Langston tonight. The mayor asked me to help with the investigation."

He looks you over with the concentration of a buyer judging medicine, then nods as though you have passed some private test. "Welcome, then. Thank you for doing this. Come to my garden for whatever you need. I want—no, I need—to know what happened to my Maybelline."

The sad smile he gives you is dazzling enough to explain several of the looks exchanged during his speech. He moves on to thank other guests.

Supper is thick onion broth, dark bread, and a heel of cheese eaten while the crowd slowly thins. The room never becomes loud. Even those who drink heavily keep their voices beneath the memory of the coffin.
