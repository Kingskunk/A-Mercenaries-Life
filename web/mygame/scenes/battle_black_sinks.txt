The column marches out at first light, four hundred strong, boots churning the frost-hardened mud into a grey soup within the first mile. Pike-shafts and crossbow cases clatter against the supply wagons. Somewhere behind you a mule screams at nothing and gets cursed back into silence. Nobody sings. Nobody talks much, past the first hour—you watch the sweat-dark back of the recruit in front of you and think about very little at all, which veteran sellswords tell you is the only way anyone survives a campaign march.

*if (prep_odessa_salve)
  *set hp_current hp_max
  The pungent herbal lanolin balm Surgeon Odessa gave you last night repels the biting marsh midges and protects your joints against boot-chafe, keeping your footing steady through the eight-hour slog.

By early afternoon the good road gives out, and the column slogs the last few miles along a raised dirt track through flat, half-drowned farmland gone to reed and standing water. The smell changes long before the causeway comes into view—wet rot, stagnant mud, and underneath it something faintly sweet that you don't let yourself think too hard about.

The Black Sinks Causeway is a massive, ancient imperial dike-road of fitted grey ashlar, originally engineered wide enough for military freight wagons but left to rot for decades. Sinking peat and seasonal floods have collapsed its outer shoulders into the marsh, leaving only a crowned centerline where heavy wagons and draft teams can crawl single-file without sliding into the waist-deep black bog. On both flanks, stagnant water chokes with dead reeds, rotting timber palisade stakes, and low, decaying stone wing-walls extending out into the fen. At the far end, where the road widens onto an elevated limestone toll apron, a squat stone gatehouse squats over the breach like a tick fat on old blood, its crenellated parapet manned by thin, ragged shapes—more scarecrow than soldier, hefting whatever passes for a weapon among people who used to be farmers and dockhands.

The company doesn't approach as one mass. The Vanguard forms up dead center on the causeway stone itself, shields locked shoulder to shoulder—first across, first to bleed, exactly the way Varren wants it. Off to either flank, barely visible against the reeds, the Scout Company is already peeling away from the column entirely, wading into the waist-deep bog to come at the gatehouse from an angle no sentry up there is watching. And well back from the stone, close enough to the baggage train to fall back if it goes wrong, the handful of Cadre recruits wait with Ysolde—too few, and worth too much, to spend on a headlong charge across open ground. Well behind them all, mounted on a dry peat knoll beneath the company's quartered raven banner, Captain Joshua Vane sits motionless in his saddle, studying the gatehouse parapet through a brass imperial spyglass while mounted runners wait on his hand signals.
