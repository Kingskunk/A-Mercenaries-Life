[b]THE DRY LION[/b]
*line_break
A dry metallic rattle shudders through the central stone pillar. The steady roar of the fountain—the background pulse that fills Conduit Square from dawn to curfew—chokes off mid-surge. One bronze lion head coughs a gout of brown clay and rust-coloured grit into a waiting bucket, wheezes on trapped air, and falls dead silent. Beside it, a second stream sputters down to a brown, dripping thread.

In the sudden acoustic void, the clatter of iron wagon tires and the shouts of distant teamsters sound jarringly loud.

A woman in a coarse, work-stained apron drops two iron-banded buckets against the stone basin with a sharp clatter, peering into the dry spout with water-bleached thumbs hooked in her belt. Across the square, a flour-dusted apprentice stands frozen in the bakehouse doorway, clutching an empty wooden proofing bowl. At the edge of the fountain curb, a man in a blue city surveyor's coat kneels over a damp line-plan spread across a timber slate, his brass municipal badge catching the grey daylight.

*label dry_lion_discovery
Tomas drops to one knee at the timber barrier, holding a horn lantern over the exposed trench as you lay out the signs: the pressure drop, the discolored seepage running east, and the lead sleeve hammered over the pipe.

He brings his chalk down along the line-plan, his brow furrowing as his finger tracks past the municipal boundary. "That branch doesn't exist on the city survey," he mutters, running his thumb over the filed-down stamp on the bronze junction. "It's an unlisted line. Someone tapped the main pipe beneath the old wall and ran water straight into the rear tenement block."

The woman with the buckets leans over the rope, her lip curling. "That yard had water when my grandmother carried flour sacks through this ward. The landlord stopped paying his line-share twenty winters back and walked away to the Patrician Quarter, but forty families still live in those rooms. We didn't dig up your street. We just kept the tap running."

*label dry_lion_setback
Tomas sets his jaw, reaching into his surveyor's satchel for a heavy iron shutoff key. "If it's unlisted, it's not approved. A broken pipe leaking into soft ground will make the whole street collapse. I seal the junction at the main line, the public fountain gets its pressure back, and the building owners can apply for a permit at Council Hall when the season turns."

From the alley mouth, a woman with curling ram-horns sweeping low along her temples steps into the square. A small child clings to the hem of her patched wool skirt, its bare feet caked in grey mud. 

"Nadi Venn," she says clearly, her voice cutting through the muttering crowd as Tomas looks up. "That pipe runs to the only tap in our court. The landlord baked the water fee into our room rents every quarter-day and left the pipe to rot beneath the flags. We've patched that joint with grease-rags and pitch every winter just to wash our children. You turn that key, and forty families don't have a cup to boil."
*set met_nadi_venn true

An elder artisan in a frayed smith's apron crouches by the lifted flags, pressing a calloused palm flat against the trembling stonework. "The packed sand under the street is washing out," he rasps. "Listen to the stones!"

Beneath your feet, a deep, grinding groan echoes through the granite paving. The soil shudders. With a sickening crack like a snapped oar, the cracked lead sleeve tears down its center. A violent surge of icy mountain water erupts from the trench, blasting a geyser of grey mud and gravel into the air. The fountain spouts let out a final, hollow wheeze and fall completely dead.

Tomas lunges back, his blue coat drenched in brown mud. "The trench is caving!" he shouts over the roar of escaping water. "If we don't fix that line now, the paving will sink around the break!"
