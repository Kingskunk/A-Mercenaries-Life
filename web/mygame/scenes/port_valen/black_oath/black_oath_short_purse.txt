The heavyset man at the foot of the stair stays behind you until you reach the landing. At the threshold, the woman in the dark woolen coat waits with one hand resting flat against the heavy oak door. She shuts it behind you, throws the iron bar into its staple, and walks past you to the slate table.

Without the usual list laid out before her, the room feels strangely hollow. A single tallow lamp flickers in the draft from the warped floorboards, throwing the hard angles of her jaw into yellow relief.

"Sit," she says.

You don't sit. Her eyes lift, settling on your face with an unhurried, clinical appraisal.

"Four months ago," she says, her voice low enough to stay beneath the rattle of the wind outside, "a runner left a two-hundred-pound collector face-down in the mud behind the canal brothel. Two punctures under the ribs. Shiv work. And six hundred silver marks vanished from an upriver strongbox before dawn."

She leans forward, resting both forearms on the slate.

"The description that came down the cut was lean, quick with a blade, and carrying the kind of nerve that makes a person run straight into an army rather than take the gallows. It took Hask four weeks to find you, and three nights of clean running to bring you to this room."

Her gaze doesn't blink. "I need to know whose boots are standing on my floor."

The woman sets the lead tin on the slate between you. It is roughly the length of a man's forearm, dull grey, its edges bound with soldered iron straps. The sliding latch on top sits loose, unfastened.

"Twenty-four copper bits missing from the boat-shed slip box," she says without preamble. "And Orren is already circling the slipway."

She taps the metal lid with the edge of her fingernail.

"Two nights ago, an unsworn barge-hand named Joram Kell drowned when a cargo barge tipped near the canal water-gates. Joram held a half-share in a two-man cargo boat moored at the Boat-Sheds slip. By custom, the boatmen pay their weekly coppers into that box. When a man drowns or breaks a limb, the fund buys out his share for his widow so the boat doesn't sit idle, and his partner takes full ownership to keep working the river."

Her eyes narrow slightly. "The buyout is twenty-four coppers, meant for Tessa Kell and her infant. When Old Corin unlocked the shed to fetch the tin tonight, the box was empty."

"Worse," she continues, "A man named Orren smells blood. He collects dock tolls for the Gilded Scales, impounding boats whenever he finds an excuse. He has already posted a notice on the slipway. If the dead man's share and mooring arrears aren't cleared with stamped coin, Orren chains the hull and tows it to the high wharves as 'unregistered abandoned tonnage.'"

The night air off the canal cut is raw and heavy with the reek of stagnant low-tide mud, rotting weed, and cold coal smoke. You leave Duckboard Market behind, stepping along the slick wooden gangways toward the boat-sheds.

Beyond Hask's driftwood scrap fence, the long row of open-sided timber sheds stands dark against the grey water. Racks of drying hemp nets hang like rotting shrouds from the crossbeams. Down on the mud-slip below, Joram Kell's cargo skiff sits wedged on greased pine rollers, a faded Gilded Scales impound writ nailed through its sternpost with an iron spike.

A yellow lantern glows faintly inside the third shed. 

Old Corin sits hunched on an upturned tar barrel, an oiled whetstone in one hand and a net-mending shuttle in the other. His knuckles are swollen with the cold, his thin white hair plastered to his forehead by mist. When his tired, watery eyes catch your boots, he flinches, dropping the shuttle onto the sawdust.

"She sent you?" he croaks, his voice trembling like a worn sail. "The woman from the shop, tell me she sent you. Orren's men are already drinking at the dock-gate tap, waiting to chain the slip. If that boat goes to the high wharves, Tessa and the little girl won't have bread for the winter."

You peer into the lock and run your thumb over the cold iron banding. While you can't tell the exact tool used, one thing is certain: the lock wasn't smashed with a hammer or split with a cold chisel. Whoever opened it took their time and didn't leave a dent on the lead casing.

"It wasn't smashed open," you say, setting the box down. "Whoever took the coin had a tool or a key, and they knew where you kept it."

Corin closes his eyes, a pained groan catching in his throat.

"Nobody comes back here except the boat crews," Corin says, his fingers knotting in his frayed woolen scarf. "The boatmen paid their bits two days ago when Joram was pulled from the canal. "Young Derry," Corin says, his voice breaking into a harsh whisper. "He's sixteen. Ran lines and rowed oars for Joram since he was twelve years old. Joram treated the lad like his own flesh and blood after Derry's mother took the fever."

Corin reaches out, clutching your sleeve with desperate strength.

"I saw him when I was walking back from the cookshop. It was just after dusk. He came tearing out from the boat-shed alley like a dog with a scalded tail, coat bundled tight around his chest. He wouldn't look at me. I called his name, and he bolted straight toward Lamp Stair!"

He looks up at you with wet, pleading eyes.

"Listen to me, runner, Derry isn't a thief. He isn't! If he took that coin, he didn't take it for dice or drink. He's terrified.
