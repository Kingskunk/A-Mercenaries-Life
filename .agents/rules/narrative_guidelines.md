## 1. Strict Subjective POV (Zero Omniscient Leaks)
* **Second-Person Subjective Anchor:** Narration is limited to what the protagonist directly sees, hears, smells, feels, or recalls.
* **No Authorial Knowledge:** Never name a distant location, enemy plan, faction title, or officer's secret past unless an NPC speaks it, a scout reports it, or the protagonist observes it in-universe.
* **Assumptions:** Never assume someone will recognize the player because of something they are wearing, how they are dressed, or any weapons they carry; with a full equipment system allowing the player to change armor, helmet, rings, cloak, boots, belt, etc., any such assumptions can be false. Never assume the character has a specific weapon type; with a weapon system where the player can switch out to bows, crossbows, quarterstaffs, axes, or swords, prose assumptions will break. This applies to the player's job or role as well: consider this an open-world life simulator rather than an assumed mercenary story—the player could be anything (a trader, a warlord, a baker, a drifter), so assuming they are a mercenary before they themselves claim or show it is a false assumption. What *can* be safely assumed and reacted to in dialogue or prose (regardless of paperdoll equipment) are starting character creation traits: Race and baseline physical appearance options.
* **Organic Name Discovery:** Never state an unfamiliar NPC's name in prose or `*choice` buttons before the protagonist hears/reads/is given it. Gate the option (`*if (met_NPC) ... *else ...`) so buttons refer to them by role, appearance, or rank until introduced.
* **No Telepathic NPC Insight:** Never state what an NPC thinks or feels omnisciently — only physical tells (voice pitch, posture, clenched jaws, avoided eye contact).
* **Progressive Codex Discovery:** Factions, locations, and lore entries are gated behind their own `*if (codex_X)` and simply omitted from the list until in-world events set that flag — no locked/undiscovered placeholder is shown, to keep the codex from cluttering up with entries the player hasn't earned yet.
* **Match Sensory Detail to the Scene's Established Vantage:** Once prose fixes the protagonist's physical distance from a place ("close enough to see, far enough to stay out of reach"), later beats in that same scene must stay within what that distance can actually reveal — sound carrying, a silhouette on a wall-walk, a lit window — not close-up dialogue or fine handwork happening behind a gate the protagonist isn't standing inside.
**No Authorial Knowledge / No Telepathic NPC Insight**: The protagonist does not see an NPC's job title or role floating over their head. They only see physical posture, visible clothing, worn tools, and what the person's hands are actively doing.
Unless backed by clear, visual, audiotory or compacting factors that made it obvious what a peron's role is Labeling generic crowd members as "off-duty lightermen, carters, and dredgers" also makes an unearned assumption about their trades. 

## 2. Ability-Specific Description (No Generic Combat Text)
* **Name the Thing, Not the Category:** A resolving cantrip, spell, weapon attack, or class feature needs its own distinct visual/auditory/physical description — never a generic stand-in like "you use your magic."
* **Every Option Gets Its Own Text:** Multiple abilities offered in one `*choice` each need their own success/failure prose tied to that ability's flavor, not a shared template with the name swapped in.
* **Dice Announcements Are Flavor Too:** The check label shown to the player should match the in-fiction action, not an abstracted mechanic name.

## 3. Continuity Discipline (No Invented Callbacks)
* **Never Reference an Event, Item, or Secret the Player Never Actually Saw:** Lines like "just as X promised" must trace to real prior text; a choice must never assume the player leveraged information or an item they never unlocked. If no such moment exists, write a generic beat ("as the plan called for") instead of inventing a false callback.
* **Never Assert an Absolute About the Player's Wider History:** The mirror-image bug — a claim like "the first thing you've eaten that someone else paid for" or "for the first time since X, the quiet is yours" asserts something *didn't* happen anywhere else across an open world the engine doesn't track, and open-world sequencing means it can go false the moment other content lets the same thing happen somewhere else first. Ground the beat in what's true of *this specific moment* (what this meal costs, what this room lacks) instead of ranking it against everything that came before. (Historical example: caught in the Rusty Anchor's stew and loft beats, since removed along with that quest — the fix was to describe the meal being free and the room being empty, not the player's whole eating/lodging history in the district.)
* **Catch-Up Option Integrity:** A fallback path to learn lore missed earlier (`*if (not(knows_X))`) must be phrased from an uninformed protagonist's perspective, never assuming the knowledge it's about to grant.
* **Cross-Squad/Cross-NPC & Rename Sweeps:** A character absent from a given squad's path shouldn't be name-dropped as if they'd interacted there. When renaming or cutting a character, search and reconcile every scene file before calling the change complete.
* **Timeless Variation Writing in Repeating Hubs:** In an open-world campaign where days, hours, and years advance dynamically—and where quests resolve, NPCs relocate, and weather shifts—every repeating hub, rest beat, and daily downtime action must feel grounded and permanently valid on Campaign Day 4 or Campaign Day 3673.
  * **The Baseline:** Ground repeatable prose in immediate physical sensation rather than fragile narrative callbacks to unfinished quests or distant NPC states.
  * **Mandatory Multi-Axis Environmental Reactivity:** Any persistent hub, lodgings, quarters, campsite, or resting location—indoor or outdoor—must never use a single static description. Arrival prose, natural lighting, exterior vantages, and ambient soundscapes must dynamically branch across active engine axes (`time_period` and `weather`).
  * **Auditory & Activity Synchronicity:** Ambient soundscapes must obey in-world operational hours. Never describe commercial bustle, artisan labor, or market sounds during periods when nearby trades and gates are shuttered.
  * **Ban Ungated Temporal Shorthand:** Time-bound words (such as references to specific parts of the day or future/past days) must never appear in unguarded repeatable text. All such phrasing must either be written neutrally or strictly gated behind the corresponding `*if (time_period = "...")` branch.
  * **Location-Derived Downtime Variety:** Resting and residential hubs must not collapse into a single monolithic rest option. Offer a structured suite of distinct physical actions derived organically from the specific privacy level, fixtures, and affordances of that unique setting, allowing different time commitments and interactions with the immediate environment.
  * **Ban Scripted NPC Micro-Actions (The "Animatronic Loop" / Groundhog Day Trap):** "To depict lively, specific human moments (a dropped crate, a dog barking, a teamster swearing) without creating an animatronic loop, use an hourly rotating pool of 4 to 6 vignettes driven by clock math: *temp vignette (((campaign_day * 24) + clock_hour) modulo 6) + 1. This guarantees that micro-actions rotate with the clock and never repeat consecutively." into an unnatural animatronic puppet loop that shatters immersion. Limit repeatable prose to continuous activity that seamlessly would fit into that area, ambient soundscapes, physical infrastructure, machinery, and environmental sensation —or dynamically randomize and branch observational vignettes if individual behavior is described.
  * **The Timelessness Litmus Test:** Before calling any repeatable choice or downtime beat complete, verify:
    1. *If the player completes every major quest in the region and returns to this cot or counter on Day 100, is any sentence in this beat false, contradictory, or awkward?*
    2. *Does this text rely on someone still being trapped, missing, or waiting downriver?*
    3. *Does this text claim an environmental condition or sound that contradicts the current time period or weather?*
    4. *Does this beat depict an individual NPC performing a specific one-off micro-action or gesture that will look like a broken animatronic loop on subsequent visits?*

## 4. Prose Craft & Vocabulary Discipline
* **Vary Vocabulary Across Adjacent Text:** Check descriptive vocabulary (verbs, adjectives, sensory words) against the current scene and the last one or two — AI writing reflexively repeats words nearby; if one was just used, pick another.
* **Substance Over Spice:** Avoid purple prose. Ground scenes in physical sensation — weather, terrain, gear weight, exhaustion, cold — so the player inhabits the moment.
* **Trust What's Shown:** Don't restate in narration what an action or line of dialogue already made clear. If a beat needs a gloss to land, fix the setup, not a tacked-on explanation.
* **Simplify Language:** Try to simplify the prose to where your average modern 20-25 year old would understand and can conceptualize.
* **Table-Facing DM Voice:** Write as though you are describing the scene aloud to one player at the table. Keep the protagonist's immediate senses and choices in the foreground, use present-tense delivery where practical, and prefer short connective beats over long authorial summaries. Let NPC dialogue, visible behavior, and discovered details carry the world's interpretation; do not explain the moral or thematic meaning of a place after the description has already shown it.
* **Jargon & Table-Facing DM Standard:** Avoid jargon. If a word requires a dictionary, a footnote, working in a specialized trade, or a parenthetical gloss to understand to the average modern day person (e.g. *herse, lunellum, municipal, berms, firkin, jetties*), it pulls the player out of the fiction. Replace these specialized terms with vivid, tactile, sensory descriptions that any player immediately pictures or can conceptualize.
* **Plain Word First (Setting Lists Are Reference, Not Vocabulary):** Period and trade word lists elsewhere (`quest/QUEST_DESIGN_RULES.md` §4 and §10) say what exists in the world, not which words to write. Use the everyday word (winch, barrel, gate, stone) unless no plain word is accurate, and use any trade term no more than once per scene. Do not reach for a word because it is period-flavored or appears on a list. Words that start to recur across scenes (flume, ledger, sealed, stamped, vellum) go into `RESTRICTED_WORDS` in `tools/lint_vocab_overuse.js`. The same applies to the Lorebook (`web/mygame/lorebook-*.js`).
* **Responsive Description Over Set Dressing:** A location should answer the player's arrival and recent action. Mention the person calling for help, the cart blocking the road, the guard watching the gate, or the rain soaking the character's sleeve before adding broad city description. When a scene pauses for a choice, end on a concrete opening the player can act on.

## 5. Natural Dialogue & Distinct NPC Voice
* **Imperfect, Not Clinical:** Dialogue should sound like a person talking — fragments, contractions, implication — not two characters explaining things they'd both already know for the player's benefit.
* **Each NPC Has a Register:** Recurring NPCs read as distinct people — word choice, sentence length, what they say vs. imply. Before writing a line, ask if it sounds like *them* based on who they are, background, social standing, rank, job or could be swapped into anyone else's mouth.

## 6. Avoid AI Prose Tics
* **Earn Silence, Don't Default to It:** Before writing that a character "says nothing," write what they actually do or say — described silence is a cop-out unless deliberate and rare.
* **Frame Actions Positively:** Avoid defining behavior by what a character *doesn't* do ("he doesn't ask why") or "Not X. Just Y." fragments — write the affirmative action instead.
* **Skip Standalone One-Word Replies:** "Yeah," "Right," "Good" as a complete response is filler dressed as an emotional beat — write what the character actually wants to say.
* **Avoid Borrowed-Comparison Shorthand:** "The way he always does" or "the kind of quiet that..." outsources specificity to a vague category — state the concrete detail directly.
* **Cut Filler Processing Gestures:** "He files it away" or "let that settle" marks information landing without showing what happens next. Cut it, or write the actual decision/shift that follows.

## 7. Describe NPCs on Introduction
* **Sensory Anchor on First Encounter:** First appearance needs a concrete physical description anchored in what the protagonist observes. Pick two or three details that tell the player who this is (build, face, one piece of clothing or gear, a scar, bearing). Do not list a full outfit or every material; a catalog of garments and trims reads as set dressing and pushes trade vocabulary into the prose.
* **Identity Follows Interaction:** Establish presence and appearance before declaring names; let identity emerge through dialogue, rank insignia, or direct introduction.

## 8. Don't Over-Explain Established Mechanics
* Once a trait/feature/mechanic is established, later uses shouldn't re-explain it — show the action and trust the player. Reserve explicit explanation for: first establishment, a moment it fails or meets friction, or a beat that specifically changes what the player learns.

## 9. Weapon-Class-Agnostic Prose (No Assumed Weapon Type)
* **Never assume a bladed weapon in shared prose not gated on class/weapon.** "Hand drops to your pommel," "bare steel from your scabbard," "grip the hilt," "blade half-drawn" are meaningless for a longbow, quarterstaff, crossbow, or arcane focus — including inside class-gated branches, since a Fighter can still have picked a spear or bow.
* **A threatening/intimidation beat should read from posture and presence, not from a hand-on-weapon gesture at all — even a correctly weapon-neutral one.** Swapping the bladed phrase for `${weapon}` ("hand resting on your ${weapon}") only fixes the physical-accuracy bug; it's still narratively off for a mage or archer, whose actual first line of defense isn't the mundane backup dagger at their belt, and for whom "resting a hand on it" doesn't read as a threat the way gripping a blade does. Write the menace as stillness, voice, or bearing instead ("you go utterly still, meeting his gaze," "radiating quiet menace," "your gaze level and unflinching") — this reads correctly for every archetype with zero weapon reference needed, and is usually the better line anyway (a real instance of this exact substitution got caught and rewritten across `alderford.txt`'s bailiff-confrontation scene).
* **The `${weapon}` variable is still the right tool for carry/proximity beats** ("your ${weapon} within arm's reach," "settling your ${weapon} across your knees") — those aren't claiming anything about how the character *uses* the weapon, just that they're carrying it. The distinction is whether the beat is about the object's presence (fine) or about it being the source of a threat (write posture instead).
* **Scan trigger:** before finalizing any passage containing *pommel*, *hilt*, *scabbard*, *bare steel*, *iron grip*, *half-drawn*, *clutching your weapon*, or a hand/grip resting "on your ${weapon}" as a threat gesture, confirm the surrounding `*if` actually gates the specific weapon (for the bladed-phrase case) and, separately, whether a posture-based rewrite serves the beat better regardless of gating.

## 10. Place Descriptions: Weather, Time & Crowds Stay in Their Own Layers
* **A location intro is four layers with one job each, so weather, time and the calendar can't contradict each other:** (1) place (constant geography and smell), (2) sky (weather and light only), (3) street life (who is about), (4) tell (a knowledgeable character's extra sentences). The flags and the mechanism live in `quest/QUEST_DESIGN_RULES.md` §12.
* **No people in the sky layer.** Rain, fog and wind sentences describe water, light, sound and smell. Vendors, ferrymen, laundry and crowds belong to the street layer, where they can be removed when the weather clears the street.
* **Match the light to the hour.** No "daylight", "sun" or "the whole district lies bright" at Night or Pre-Dawn, and no sun under Overcast or Fog.
* **When weather empties the street, say so, and let the calendar show as absence.** A storm on Marketday is "the stalls are lashed shut", not a crowd. Never describe a full market, a packed plaza or laborers crowding the lanes in severe weather.
* **Never assert weather in a calendar or activity line.** A Greyday paragraph must not say "grey light", and a Marketday line must not hedge ("if it has rained this morning"). Weather lives in the sky layer only.
* **Don't print the weather variable mid-sentence.** `${weather}` prints a capitalized type name ("the Storm"); write "the weather" or branch on a flag.
* **Sheltered and indoor places skip the sky layer** and get at most one muffled weather line (rain on the roof, fog against the porthole glass).
